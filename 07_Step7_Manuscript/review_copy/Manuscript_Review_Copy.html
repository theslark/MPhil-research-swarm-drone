Abstract—Decentralized unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) swarms used for search and rescue lose contact with their members for two very different reasons: drones fly out of communication range, or drones crash. Existing decentralized allocators handle either network partitions or known failures, but not both. In practice the two cases are indistinguishable to the remaining drones: a peer has simply gone silent. Reassigning too early duplicates work and creates conflicting mission state when a live drone returns; reassigning too late leaves the tasks of a crashed drone unserved. This paper proposes a self-healing task allocation architecture that treats this ambiguity explicitly. Each UAV keeps a replica of a mission ledger built from conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs), which records task completion, ownership claims and evidence of life, and which converges deterministically after network partitions and after wrong failure suspicions. A suspicion estimator combines accrual failure detection with each peer's published plan, and a takeover policy, either a threshold rule or a multi-agent proximal policy optimization (MAPPO) policy trained with centralized training and decentralized execution, decides when a suspected peer's task should be claimed. We formalize the problem, prove that the ledger replicas converge and agree on task ownership, and pre-specify a simulation-based evaluation with eleven scenarios, seven methods including the recent CC-OPI allocator, ten ablations, and a statistical analysis plan. The central quantity studied is the trade-off between task recovery time and duplicated work. Experimental results are pending and are not reported here.

Index Terms—UAV swarms, decentralized task allocation, intermittent connectivity, failure detection, self-healing systems, conflict-free replicated data types, multi-agent reinforcement learning, search and rescue.

I. INTRODUCTION

Swarms of small unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) can cover a disaster area much faster than a single aircraft, provided that the swarm divides the search area among its members and keeps doing so as conditions change [1]. In realistic deployments, however, inter-UAV links are short-ranged and lossy, so the swarm repeatedly fragments into groups that cannot communicate [2], [3], and individual UAVs fail through battery depletion, collisions or hardware faults.

Decentralized task allocation has responded to each of these problems separately. Market-based algorithms such as the consensus-based bundle algorithm (CBBA) provide conflict-free assignments without a central planner [4], [5], and a growing body of work makes them robust to packet loss, limited bandwidth and limited range [6], [7], [8], [9]. The recent CC-OPI allocator explicitly handles swarms that fragment into “information islands” and merges completion records when islands meet [10]. Separately, replanning methods reassign the tasks of UAVs whose failure is known [11], [12].

These two lines of work rest on assumptions that cannot hold at the same time. Partition-tolerant allocators assume that UAVs do not crash (CC-OPI states that “no mid-mission UAV failure are modeled” [10]); failure-replanning methods assume that failures are known events, typically with global communication [12]. In a real swarm, a silent UAV may be crashed or merely out of range, and distributed-systems theory shows that no observer can reliably tell the two apart in an asynchronous network [13], [14]. Acting on this ambiguity is costly in either direction: a premature takeover duplicates work when the “failed” UAV returns, while a delayed takeover leaves the tasks of a crashed UAV unserved. This dilemma between fault correction and fault tolerance has been measured in generic decentralized systems [15], but not, to our knowledge, in UAV task allocation.

This paper addresses that gap. Our contributions are:

  1. A formal problem statement for decentralized UAV task allocation under combined network partitions and crash-stop failures, with ground-truth metrics for duplicated work, false takeovers and censored recovery times (Section III).
  2. A convergent mission ledger built from conflict-free replicated data types [16], [17] that extends completion-only merging [10] to ownership and evidence-of-life state, together with proofs that replicas converge and agree on task ownership (Section IV-B).
  3. A plan-aware suspicion estimator that adapts accrual failure detection [18] to multi-hop, intermittent evidence and uses each peer's published plan to separate “silent and expected far away” from “silent and expected nearby” (Section IV-C).
  4. A takeover policy formulated as a decentralized partially observable Markov decision process and trained with MAPPO [19] under centralized training and decentralized execution, compared against a tunable threshold rule (Section IV-E).
  5. A pre-specified, reproducible evaluation protocol: eleven scenarios, seven methods including a fair adaptation of CC-OPI, ten ablations, and a statistical analysis plan (Section V).

Because the experiments have not yet been run, this manuscript is a methodology paper: it presents the problem, the design, the proofs and the evaluation protocol, and marks every place where experimental data will be required. No experimental result is reported or implied.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section II reviews related work and states the research gap. Section III formalizes the problem. Section IV presents the architecture and its components. Section V details the experimental design. Section VI defines the results to be reported. Section VII discusses limitations and future work, and Section VIII concludes.

II. RELATED WORK AND RESEARCH GAP

The literature was searched in Crossref, arXiv and the open web using 21 topic queries (2019–2026) supplemented by foundational works; 708 unique records were screened and 72 retained, with metadata verified against Crossref or the publisher. Most evidence is at abstract level; full texts were examined for the closest works [10], [12], [20], [21].

A. Decentralized Task Allocation

Gerkey and Matarić formalized multi-robot task allocation (MRTA) and its taxonomy [22]. CBBA combines market-based bundle construction with a consensus phase and is proven to converge to conflict-free assignments [4]; its asynchronous variant ACBBA removes synchronous rounds [5]. CBBA variants have been applied to search and rescue [23] and to large swarms [24], and a unified comparison of centralized and distributed allocators, including resilience to UAV failures, is available [25].

B. Allocation under Imperfect Communication

Communication-aware CBBA learns to schedule messages under bandwidth limits and collisions [6]; DTBA-C3 compensates for packet loss and data errors [7]; LR-PI resolves conflicts under constrained communication [26]; AC-DMTA suppresses messages that cannot change the assignment under stale information [27]; and UCDG-PD uses chance constraints and task-local leases under stochastic links [8]. Auctions degrade gracefully under lossy auctioneer–bidder links when winner data are re-sent [28], and an empirical study compares decentralized allocators under Bernoulli, Gilbert–Elliott and fading channels [9]. Closest to our setting, CC-OPI interleaves negotiation with execution in a swarm fragmented by a short communication radius, merging completed-task sets by union when islands meet and reporting “wasted distance” as the price of robustness [10]. CC-OPI explicitly excludes mid-mission UAV failures and assumes loss-free links within range.

C. Failure-Triggered Reallocation and Self-Healing

ES-CBBA escalates the replanning scope after UAV failures [11]; performance-impact and auction variants reallocate tasks after single or multiple failures [29], [30]; and IRDS reallocates search sectors by reverse auction after injected battery faults, reporting high mission success under workforce loss [12]. In IRDS the market state is global and failure is “treated instantaneously as a market event” [12]. Self-healing work on UAV swarms more often targets formation or topology: recursive grouping restores configuration after individual failures [31], and graph-based deep reinforcement learning restores connectivity [32]. Meeting-based patrolling detects failures at rendezvous points and redistributes responsibilities, but reports cascading failures under some fault conditions [33].

D. Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning

Value-decomposition methods (VDN [34], QMIX [35]) and actor–critic methods (MADDPG [36], MAPPO [19]) follow the centralized-training, decentralized-execution (CTDE) paradigm, whereas independent learners (IQL [37], IPPO [38]) and networked actor–critic methods [39] learn without a centralized component. MARL has been applied to UAV task allocation with communication gating [40], graph embeddings [41] and asynchronous value decomposition combined with ACBBA [42]. MAGEC trains a shared graph-neural-network policy with MAPPO for patrolling under agent attrition and message loss [20]; failures are not detected explicitly, and crashed and disconnected agents are not distinguished. MAPPO's robustness to partial agent failure depends strongly on the reward structure [43].

E. Distributed-Systems Foundations

Consensus is impossible in asynchronous systems with even one crash [13]; unreliable failure detectors characterize what can be achieved [14]; and accrual detectors output a continuous suspicion level rather than a binary verdict [18]. Gossip-style detectors spread heartbeat counters epidemically [44], and SWIM separates failure detection from membership dissemination [45]. Under partitions, a replicated system must trade consistency for availability [46]. Conflict-free replicated data types (CRDTs) remain available under partition and converge deterministically [16], [17]; delta-state CRDTs reduce synchronization traffic [47], including in opportunistic networks [48], [49]. Delay-tolerant networking supplies the store-carry-forward model [50], [51], [52].

III. PROBLEM FORMULATION

A. Mission and Agents

The search area is divided into M sectors, each treated as a task k∈K={1,…,M} with centre qk, service time sk>0 and weight wk>0. The task set is fixed at mission start, and each task requires one UAV (single-task robots, single-robot tasks, time-extended assignment [22]). A task is complete once one UAV has serviced it for sk; any further service is duplicated work.

A homogeneous swarm N={1,…,N} flies at speed v under point-mass kinematics. UAV i has position pi(t), energy ei(t) and true status σi(t)∈{alive,crashed}; σi is known only to the simulator and is used solely for evaluation. Energy evolves as

ei(t+Δ t)=ei(t)-cf Δ t 1[active]-cm nitx(t), (1)

where nitx(t) is the number of messages transmitted in the step. UAVs know their own position and share a loosely synchronized clock (GNSS time) with bounded skew ϵ.

B. Communication Model

The communication graph is G(t)=(Nalive(t),E(t)) with (i,j)∈E(t) iff ‖pi(t)-pj(t)‖≤ Rc. A message over a link of length d is delivered with probability

q(d)=q0(1-(d/Rc)γ), d≤ Rc, (2)

optionally replaced by a two-state Gilbert–Elliott burst-loss channel [9]. The connected components C1(t),…,Cc(t)(t) of G(t) are the partitions. There is no infrastructure; information crosses partitions only when UAVs physically carry it (store-carry-forward [50]).

C. Failure Model

Failures are crash-stop: at time tjc UAV j stops moving and transmitting permanently. Byzantine behaviour and partial degradation are out of scope. A live UAV may also be silent for a long time (out of range, in another partition, or under a radio blackout) and later reconnect. A silent peer is therefore either crashed or disconnected, and the observing UAV cannot know which.

D. Objectives

The swarm should maximize the weighted mission completion rate while limiting duplicated work. Let 1ik(t) indicate that UAV i is servicing task k at time t, Sk=Σi∫0Tmax1ik(t) dt the total service spent on k, and Kdone the tasks completed by Tmax. The two primary metrics are

MCR=(Σk∈Kdonewk)/(Σk∈Kwk),(3)

DWR=(Σk∈Kdone(Sk-sk))/(Σk∈Ksk). (4)

For each task k left unfinished by a UAV j that crashes at tjc (an orphaned task), the recovery time is τkcl=tkclaim-tjc, where tkclaim is the first claim on k by another live UAV. Recovery times are right-censored at Tmax when no claim occurs.

E. Research Questions and Hypotheses

  • RQ1: When UAVs cannot tell a crashed peer from a disconnected one, what is the trade-off between recovery time and duplicated work? H2: both vary monotonically with takeover aggressiveness.
  • RQ2: Can a convergent ledger keep mission state consistent across partitions and wrong suspicions, and at what communication cost?
  • RQ3: Does a learned takeover policy improve on a tuned threshold rule? H3: yes under intermittent connectivity, but not under full connectivity.
  • RQ4: How does the complete design compare with centralized, CBBA-based and CC-OPI allocation when partitions and failures co-occur? H1: it completes more of the mission when failures occur and is non-inferior when they do not.
  • RQ5: How do results scale with swarm size, range and failure rate, and does a per-partition coordinator help? H4: the coordinator gives no significant benefit.
  • H5: the plan-aware suspicion term lowers the false-takeover rate at equal recovery time.

IV. PROPOSED ARCHITECTURE

A. Design Principles and Overview

The architecture follows five principles: (i) every UAV holds a full replica of the mission state and can complete tasks with no peer contact; (ii) contacts improve coordination but their absence never blocks progress, i.e., availability is preferred over consistency under partition [46]; (iii) correctness resides in a convergent data structure, not in the network, so learning affects efficiency but never consistency; (iv) silent peers are suspected to a degree, never declared dead [18]; and (v) no permanent leader exists. Fig. 1 illustrates the setting and Fig. 2 the onboard components, which are identical on every UAV.

Fig. 1. System setting. The swarm forms and loses partitions as UAVs move; information crosses partitions only through store-carry-forward. An isolated live UAV (U6) and a crashed UAV (U7) are indistinguishable to their peers.

Fig. 2. Onboard agent stack. The mission ledger (M2) is the only merged state; suspicion (M1) is a local view. The takeover policy (M3) overrides the base allocator (M4) only for tasks whose owner is suspected. The group coordinator (M5) is used only in an ablation.

B. Mission Ledger (M2)

Each UAV i holds a replica Li=(Di,Hi,CLi,MEMi). Every component is a join-semilattice whose merge ⊔ is commutative, associative and idempotent, and every local update is an inflation (it moves the state upward).

Definition (Ledger components):

  1. Completions Di⊆K×N×ℝ, a grow-only set merged by union.
  2. Evidence of life Hi:N→(c,τ,p,π): for each UAV j a heartbeat counter c, timestamp τ, position p and published plan π. Only j increments cj; entries are merged by keeping the larger counter.
  3. Claims CLi:K×N→(ε,ρ,r)∈ℕ×[0,1]×ℕ: entry (k,j) is written only by UAV j and holds a claim epoch εkj, progress ρkj and release epoch rkj; the claim is active iff εkj>rkj. Entries are merged component-wise by max.
  4. Membership MEMi=(Ai,Ri), a two-phase set; planned departures enter Ri, crashes never do.

Local updates by UAV j on task k are: claim εkj← 1+maxℓεkℓ; progress ρkj←max(ρkj,new progress); release rkj←εkj; and reassert, which is a claim permitted only when ρkj≥ρmin and ρkj strictly exceeds the progress of the current owner. The current owner of task k in replica i is

oi(k)=argmaxj: εkj>rkj\ (εkj, -j), (5)

i.e., the active claim with the highest epoch, ties broken by the lowest identifier.

Assumption 1 (Temporal connectivity): There exists W<∞ such that, for every interval of length W, the union of G(t) over the interval connects all UAVs alive throughout it.

Assumption 2 (Sufficient contacts): Any two UAVs in contact eventually complete a digest-and-delta exchange (possibly over several contacts), since the ledger size is bounded by O(M N) and bandwidth per contact is positive.

Lemma 1: The ledger state space, ordered component-wise, is a join-semilattice with join ⊔, and every local update is an inflation.

Proof: A grow-only set under union and a two-phase set under component-wise union are join-semilattices [16]. For H, each entry j is written only by j, whose counter strictly increases with every write; hence the map from counter values to tuples is a function, and “keep the larger counter” is the join of a totally ordered set lifted to the tuple. For CL, each entry is a product of totally ordered sets under component-wise max, which is a join-semilattice; a map of join-semilattices under point-wise join is a join-semilattice, and a finite product of join-semilattices is a join-semilattice. Claim, progress, release and reassert only increase entry components, and heartbeat and completion updates only add or increase, so all updates are inflations. ∎

Proposition 1 (Convergence): Two replicas that have received the same set of updates are equal, regardless of the order, duplication or delay of delivery. Under Assumptions 1 and 2, every update reaches every UAV that remains alive, so all live replicas converge.

Proof: By Lemma 1, each replica's state equals the join of the initial state and the states produced by all updates it has received. Because ⊔ is commutative, associative and idempotent, this join is independent of order and multiplicity, which is the strong-eventual-consistency property of state-based CRDTs [16]. For delivery, consider an update created by UAV u at time t0 and a UAV x alive throughout [t0,t0+W]. By Assumption 1 there is a time-respecting sequence of contacts from u to x within the union graph; by Assumption 2 each contact in the sequence eventually transfers all state that the receiver lacks, including the update, because merged state is retained and re-offered at every subsequent contact. By induction along the sequence, x eventually receives the update. ∎

Proposition 2 (Owner agreement): The owner function (5) is well defined, and any two replicas with equal CL components assign the same owner to every task.

Proof: Identifiers are unique, so the pairs (εkj,-j) are totally ordered and the maximum over a finite non-empty set is unique; if no claim is active, the task has no owner. The function depends only on CL, so equal CL implies equal owners. ∎

Two remarks delimit these results. First, suspicion values are not part of the ledger: they influence which updates a UAV issues, never how updates merge, so Propositions 1 and 2 hold irrespective of detector quality. Second, replicas may disagree between contacts; the duplicated work caused by such transient disagreement is precisely what the evaluation measures. If Assumption 1 fails because groups never meet again, no protocol can reconcile them, a limitation shared with CC-OPI [10].

Synchronization:

On each new contact, UAVs exchange heartbeats and a compact digest of their ledgers, then send missing deltas [47]: first claims and completions that concern tasks of suspected peers, then all remaining deltas, subject to a per-contact bandwidth cap. Lost messages are harmless because the next digest exchange re-offers missing state and duplicate deliveries are absorbed by idempotence.

C. Suspicion Estimator (M1)

For UAV i observing peer j, let τij(t) be the timestamp of the freshest evidence of life for j in Hi, whether received directly or relayed, and gij(t)=t-τij(t) the current silence. Following the accrual principle [18],

φij(t)=-log10(1-F̂j(gij(t))), (6)

where F̂j is the estimated distribution of evidence gaps for j: a mixture of a prior F0 fitted for the communication setting and the empirical distribution of j's observed gaps, weighted by n/(n+n0) with n observed gaps. Unlike classical accrual detectors, gaps here are dominated by mobility and partitions rather than by network jitter.

The ledger also holds j's published plan, from which i predicts j's position hatpj(t). Let ηij(t)=1[‖pi(t)-hatpj(t)‖≤βRc] indicate that j is expected to be in range, and let mij(t) be the time during the current silence for which ηij=1. The combined suspicion is

ψij(t)=φij(t)+κ (mij(t))/(Thb), κ≥ 0, (7)

with heartbeat period Thb. Silence while expected far away increases suspicion slowly; silence while expected nearby increases it quickly. Hypothesis H5 tests whether this term improves discrimination between crashed and disconnected peers; κ=0 is an ablation.

D. Base Allocator (M4)

Within each connected group, UAVs run an event-driven asynchronous CBBA [4], [5]. The path score is Si(πi)=Σk∈πiwkλdτik(πi) with time discount λd∈(0,1), and bundles are built from tasks that are not complete and not owned by an unsuspected peer. Re-bundling is triggered by topology changes, completions, new claims or changes in task availability, in the spirit of CC-OPI's event-driven replanning [10]. Allocation outcomes are written as ledger claims, so allocation and takeover share a single ownership record. Tasks owned by suspected peers are not bid on automatically; they are passed to the takeover policy.

E. Takeover Policy (M3)

A task k is an orphan candidate for UAV i if it is incomplete and its owner oi(k) has ψi,oi(k)≥Φopen. The takeover decision is formulated as a decentralized partially observable Markov decision process ⟨N,S,{Ai},P,R,{Ωi},O,γd⟩:

  • Observation oi: own position, energy and bundle length; features of the K highest-ranked orphan candidates (relative position, wk, sk, owner suspicion ψ, silence g, owner's last progress, number of active claimants); and up to L neighbours. Sets are encoded by attention pooling [53], making the input independent of N and M.
  • Actions Ai={continue,hold,claim(1),…,claim(K)} with masking of invalid options.
  • Decision epochs: event-triggered by suspicion level crossings, new orphans or task completion, with a periodic fallback; rewards between epochs are accumulated.
  • Team reward
    rt=Σk∈Ktnew(wk-λ Dupk)-λΣkpostk(t)-μΣiΔ ei(t), (8)
    where Ktnew are tasks completed at t, Dupk is the service spent on k by UAVs other than the completer (charged at completion), and postk(t) is service at t on an already completed task. Both terms are causal.

The policy is trained with MAPPO [19] using a shared actor for all UAVs and a centralized critic Vψ(s) that, during training only, observes the true status of every UAV. This privileged critic is discarded at execution, so deployment remains fully decentralized. To obtain the whole trade-off curve from one training run, λ is sampled per episode and supplied as an input to actor and critic. We chose MAPPO over QMIX and VDN because value-decomposition mixers either assume a fixed team size or cannot represent “exactly one UAV should take over”; IPPO [38] serves as an ablation to test the value of the centralized critic. As a non-learned baseline, the threshold rule claims the nearest orphan whose owner has ψ≥Φ, and sweeping Φ traces its trade-off curve.

F. Failure Recovery, Isolation and Optional Coordinator

Fig. 3 shows the recovery workflow. If the suspected peer has crashed, the claimant completes the task and self-healing is achieved. If the peer was alive, merging resolves the conflict deterministically: the returning UAV reasserts if its progress is at least ρmin and exceeds the claimant's progress, and otherwise yields. Fig. 4 gives a concrete example.

Fig. 3. Failure-recovery workflow. Both error types are measured: wasted or duplicated work after a false suspicion, and delayed claiming when the peer has truly crashed.

Fig. 4. Illustrative example (not an experimental result). U3 loses contact, completes k7 alone, and is suspected by U5, which claims k8 with a higher epoch. When contact resumes, U3 has progressed further on k8 and reasserts; U5 yields and its travel is counted as wasted.

A UAV without contact follows an isolation policy: it finishes its own bundle, then claims the nearest task that is open in its own replica, periodically diverts toward the last known position of the largest group to attempt a rendezvous, and returns to base when its remaining energy covers only the return flight plus a margin. For the leader ablation (H4), each connected group derives a coordinator deterministically from the ledger (the unsuspected member with the most energy, ties broken by identifier) holding a renewable lease. Unlike Raft [54], a minority partition still obtains a coordinator; concurrent coordinators are resolved by lease epoch when groups merge, and ownership is always decided by (5).

C. Methods, Baselines and Ablations

The compared methods are: B0, a centralized oracle with global communication and instant failure knowledge (upper bound); B1, greedy nearest-task (lower bound); B2, CBBA/ACBBA with timeout-based reassignment [4], [5]; B3a, CC-OPI re-implemented as published [10]; B3b, CC-OPI with the same timeout rule as B2, a fair adaptation to the failure setting; P1, the proposed design with the threshold rule; and P2, the proposed design with MAPPO. The CC-OPI re-implementation must reproduce its published completion rate in its own setting (within five percentage points) before use. Ablations remove or replace one component at a time: completion-only merge (A1), binary timeout instead of accrual suspicion (A2), κ=0 (A3), epoch-first and progress-first owner rules (A4, A5), first-in-first-out instead of prioritized exchange (A6), full-state instead of delta synchronization (A7), per-group coordinator (A8), IPPO instead of MAPPO (A9), and MAPPO without the privileged critic (A10). Each method's key parameter is tuned on separate validation seeds with an equal budget of eight grid points.

MAPPO is trained on N∈{6,10,16}, Rc∈{200,400,800}, crash fractions in [0,0.3] and random blackouts and partitions, with five independent training seeds, and evaluated on unseen seeds and on N∈{24,32}.

D. Metrics

Beyond MCR (3) and DWR (4), the evaluation reports the following.

  • Time and allocation efficiency: completion time Tmc; coverage AUC=Tmax-1∫0TmaxMCR(t) dt; and allocation efficiency relative to the oracle's discounted score.
  • Waste and recovery: wasted-distance ratio [10]; claim latency τcl and service-resumption time; and the fraction of orphaned tasks never recovered.
  • Error rates: false-takeover rate FTR=#{claims while previous owner alive}/#{claims} and its cost.
  • Detector quality: the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) of ψ over silent pairs labelled crashed or alive (S10).
  • Ledger consistency: owner divergence Div(t), the fraction of co-located replica pairs and tasks with differing owners; and time to consistency after partition merges.
  • Network: packet delivery ratio; update dissemination latency (mean and 95th percentile, censored); mean component count; and pairwise connectivity.
  • Cost and scalability: messages and bytes per completed task; energy per completed task; autonomy during isolation (tasks completed while isolated relative to tasks owned); leader recovery time (A8); and decision computation time versus N.

The trade-off between median τcl and DWR is summarized by the normalized hypervolume of each method's front.

E. Statistical Analysis Plan

Each configuration is evaluated on 30 seeds (validation and training seeds are disjoint). Paired comparisons use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test [55] with Holm correction within each hypothesis family [56]; effect sizes are matched-pairs rank-biserial correlations with 95% bootstrap confidence intervals. Recovery times are analysed with Kaplan–Meier estimators [57], log-rank tests and restricted mean survival time, because unrecovered tasks are censored. MARL results are aggregated across training seeds with interquartile means and stratified bootstrap intervals [58]. Table IV lists the decision rule for each hypothesis; H1 includes a non-inferiority margin of two percentage points in failure-free scenarios. Thirty paired seeds give approximately 80% power for a standardized paired effect of about 0.55; if pilot variance is higher, the seed count will be increased before the main runs.

B. Interpretation Plan

Each hypothesis can fail, and failures will be reported. If H3 is not supported, the learned policy offers no advantage over a tuned threshold, which would indicate that the trade-off is governed mainly by the suspicion signal rather than by the decision rule; the ledger and detector contributions would remain. If H5 is not supported, the plan-aware term will be removed from the recommended design. If the proposed design is not non-inferior to CC-OPI in failure-free scenarios, the overhead of ownership and evidence replication will be quantified and discussed as the cost of failure awareness.

VII. LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK

The study is simulation-based and abstracts flight dynamics, collision avoidance and wind; the channel model uses disk connectivity with distance-dependent and bursty loss but no medium-access contention, a limitation shared with CC-OPI [10]. Only crash-stop failures are modelled; Byzantine and degraded behaviour are excluded. The task set is fixed and tasks are atomic and single-UAV; dynamic task arrival, heterogeneous UAVs and multi-UAV tasks are natural extensions. Convergence relies on temporal connectivity (Assumption 1) and loosely synchronized clocks, whose sensitivity is tested in S11. The literature review used Crossref, arXiv and web search and examined several works only at abstract level; confirmatory searches in IEEE Xplore and Scopus and full-text checks of [33], [59] remain to be done. Future work includes validation of the channel model in ns-3, software-in-the-loop flight simulation, hardware experiments, and extending the plan-aware detector with learned motion models.

VIII. CONCLUSION

This paper identified a gap between partition-tolerant and failure-aware task allocation for UAV swarms: in practice a silent UAV may be crashed or merely disconnected, and acting on that ambiguity trades recovery time against duplicated work. We proposed a self-healing architecture in which a CRDT-based mission ledger guarantees convergence of completion and ownership state after partitions and wrong suspicions, a plan-aware accrual detector grades suspicion, and a threshold or MAPPO takeover policy decides when to act. We proved convergence and owner agreement under stated assumptions and pre-specified a reproducible evaluation with baselines, ablations and statistical tests. The experimental evaluation is the next step; its results will determine which of the stated hypotheses hold.
